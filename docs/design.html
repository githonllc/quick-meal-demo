Quick Meal Demo Design

A mobile web demo of a DoorDash concept. Built for university students in the Bay Area and across the US. A student opens Quick Meal, says how much time they have, can add a budget, and sees which meals fit. If nothing fits, the app shows the closest meals and a one-tap way to loosen the search.

Design variant

Design in short
  • Principle: decision time = decision steps × cost of each step. Quick Meal gives the student enough facts to make each step cheap, but it does not decide for them. See "Why Quick Meal informs and does not decide" below.
  • Screens: a thin Home, the Quick Meal list, a Filters sheet, a restaurant menu in budget view, and a price breakdown sheet.
  • Goal: help a busy student decide fast. A student with 20 minutes between study blocks needs to know which meals they can get in time. Success is a shorter time to place an order. Orders that arrive within the chosen window are the guardrail.
  • Time comes first: "How much time do you have?" It filters on the estimated delivery time, from order to door. Times are estimates, not promises, shown as a range like "Est. 9–14 min".
  • Budget means the estimated all-in price of one meal: food, fees, tax and tip, so the total at checkout is no surprise.
  • Results are restaurant cards (an experiment in version 37): the place, its stars, the estimated time and distance, and which meals fit, like "5 under $20: Soup Dumplings (8), …". The card opens the restaurant's menu, where every meal shows its all-in price.
  • Delivery or pickup, not both: one switch in the Filters sheet. Delivery filters by time, pickup filters by distance. Only one of the two is on at a time (P7). Pickup cards show the distance and no time, and the all-in prices on the menu have no delivery fee.
  • Every sort is visible: Fastest (the default), Lowest price and Top rated for delivery; Nearest takes the place of Fastest for pickup. Each one sorts by a number on the card or the menu. There is no "Best match".
  • Evidence: each decision cites the team's press release, concept thesis and Worksheet 2. Those documents use the concept's earlier name, Busy Mode; Quick Meal is the current name.
  • Cuisine tabs across the top (All, Chinese, Indian …), one at a time. Chip row: Filters, then time (or distance for pickup), then a budget slider. Sort lives in the full Filters sheet.
  • No match is a designed state. It shows the closest meals, says how each one misses, and offers chips like "Budget up to $17 · 1 result".
Design principle

Why Quick Meal informs and does not decide

Decision time=Decision steps×Cost of each step

Our north star is time to place an order (P9). A student spends that time in steps: set the time, set the budget, pick a cuisine, pick a meal, check the price. Each step costs time and effort. Quick Meal makes the steps fewer and each one cheaper. It gives the student the facts they need at each step. It does not make the choice for them.

Fewer steps

  • Saved default: set "30 min · $20" once (P8).
  • All-in price on every menu row: no trip to checkout to see the total (P4).
  • One-tap relax chips when nothing fits.
  • Delivery or pickup: one switch, so one question about speed, not two (P7).

Cheaper steps

  • Estimated all-in price, with a breakdown one tap away.
  • Time as a range, "Est. 9–14 min".
  • Stars and the meals that fit on the card; dish like rates on the menu (P5).
  • Near meals say how they miss: "$1.32 over budget".
  • The menu shows what fits and what is just over (P6).

What Quick Meal does not do

  • It does not pick a meal for the student.
  • It does not hide meals that are just over budget.
  • It does not loosen a filter without a tap.
  • No hidden ranking: every sort uses a number shown on the card or the menu.
  • Sort only orders the list. The dish line and the full menu keep every option one tap away.

Why not decide for the student? Food is a very personal choice: taste, mood, diet and craving change from meal to meal. A guess that is right saves one step. A guess that is wrong adds steps: the student reads the pick, rejects it, then searches anyway, now with less trust in the app. In a 20-minute gap that is the worst case. A fact the student does not use costs nothing. A pick they must undo costs a step and some trust. So Quick Meal narrows the list with the student's own limits (time, budget, cuisine) and shows facts. The student makes the choice.

Product decisions

How Quick Meal works

These hold for the real product, in any city.

P1

Entry point

Decision
First item in the Home category row, left of Deals. Red tile, white lightning icon, a NEW tag. The banner also opens Quick Meal. Other Home items show "Not part of this demo.", because category and restaurant pages are out of scope.
Why
The row scrolls sideways and only about five items fit on screen. First place is always visible. The rest of Home is white with plain line icons, as in the current DoorDash app, so one red tile stands out. It also matches the app icon.
Evidence
  • PRthey can switch on [Quick Meal] with one tap. The tile is that one tap; saved filters (P8) do the rest.
  • No source for the exact place on Home. That part follows the current DoorDash Home.
P2

Filter controls

Layout
Two rows under the title. First the chip row: Filters · Time (or Distance, for pickup) · Budget. Then the cuisine tabs, right above the list. Sort is only in the full Filters sheet, which runs Delivery | Pickup with its steps · Budget · Sort.
Why this order
Filters set the pool of meals that fit. A tab then shows one cuisine from that pool. Tabs sit next to the list they switch, the usual place for tabs. Changing a filter keeps the current tab.
Not DoorDash's order
DoorDash puts store-type tabs above filters because the tab decides which filters apply. Here the filters decide which meals fit, and the cuisine tab only splits them, so filters go first. With filters under the tabs, they would look like they belong to one tab, and users would wonder whether switching tabs keeps or resets them. With filters on top, it is clear they stay the same across every tab.
Budget
Slider for the maximum all-in price per meal. Range $10 to $40, in $1 steps. The value shows above the thumb: "Up to $20". The far right end reads "$40+ (any)" and means no budget filter.
Time
The Delivery side of the switch, and the default. The sheet asks "How much time do you have?" and says "Estimated arrival within this window. Not guaranteed." A slider with stops 15 · 20 · 30 · 45 min · Any. It snaps to a stop, and its value shows above the thumb: "Up to 30 min", or "Any time" at the far right, which means no time filter. It means the estimated time from order to door. Students subtract their own eating time; the team agreed the window does not include it. Whether students read the question this way is checked in a later user study.
Minutes, not a clock time
Steps and cards use minutes ("Est. 9–14 min"), never a clock time ("by 6:24 PM"). The estimate starts when the order is placed, and the app cannot know how long the student will look before ordering. A clock time would slip while they look, and that browsing time is what we want to cut. Minutes stay true at any moment and use the same unit as the filter. A clock time fits after the order, when the estimate has a start.
Sliders
Time (or distance) and budget use the same slider, so the two main limits look and work alike, and time is no longer the smaller control. A teammate had asked for a bigger time control. The time slider snaps to a few stops: a time window is a rough choice, and the stops keep the URL, the relax chips and the counts the same as with tap steps. Version 35 used tap steps here.
Distance
The Pickup side of the switch (P7). It asks "How far can you go?" with the same kind of slider: "Within 1 mi", or "Any distance" at the far right. Its stops depend on the user's location. A dense area like downtown San Jose gets 0.5 · 1 · 2 · 3 mi · Any. A spread-out suburb gets 1 · 2 · 3 · 5 mi · Any.
Cuisine
Tabs: All · Chinese · Indian · Mexican · Pizza · Burgers · Healthy · Fast Food · Sushi. One at a time; All means any cuisine. The row scrolls sideways. A place can carry several tags, since Healthy and Fast Food are tags rather than cuisines, so one place can appear under more than one tab. Tabs show no counts. The Filters badge does not count the tab, because the selected tab is already visible. Applying filters keeps the current tab.
Sort
Delivery: Fastest · Lowest price · Top rated. Pickup: Nearest · Lowest price · Top rated. The first one is the default. Only in the full sheet. Fastest, Nearest and Top rated use a number on the card: the time, the distance or the stars. Lowest price orders places by their cheapest meal that fits; that meal comes first in the dish line, and its price is on the menu. Each side sorts by speed in its own unit: time for delivery, distance for pickup. Pickup cards show no time, so Pickup has no Fastest. For delivery the time already includes the trip (P7), so Delivery has no Nearest. Switching sides swaps Fastest and Nearest; Lowest price and Top rated stay. Version 37 renamed Most liked to Top rated, because cards no longer show a dish like rate; an old sort=liked link opens with Top rated.
No "Best match"
Version 35 removed it. It mixed like rate, stars and time in a way the student could not see, so it looked like a pick made for them. A sort the student can read from the card keeps the order honest.
Chip tap
Each chip opens a small sheet for that one filter. The Time chip reads "30 min" for delivery or "Pickup · 1 mi" for pickup, and its sheet has the same Delivery | Pickup switch and slider. The Time and Budget sheets both show a "Show results" button, so a drag does not reload the list at every stop or dollar. A tab applies at once.
No live count
The button reads "Show results" and the search runs only when the user applies. A live count needs a request on every change, and a slow answer can show a stale number (for example "Show 0 results" that then shows meals). The count line on the list shows the number after applying.
Why
Time comes first because the job is fitting a meal into a gap in the day. Budget is a number students already have in mind, often not a round one, so a slider with $1 steps fits it. Time and distance are rough choices, so their sliders snap to a few stops. Delivery and pickup share one chip, so the chip row stays short. Cuisine answers "what am I in the mood for", which usually has one answer. A tab makes that one tap instead of three.
Principle
Filters use only the student's own limits. Quick Meal never adds a limit the student did not set, such as a guessed cuisine.
Evidence
  • PRTime first: students just tell [Quick Meal] how much time they have; a restaurant would only appear if it can make the time window.
  • WS2 BThe job: have dinner in the 20 minutes between study blocks. WS2 DC1 Capture user selected available meal time, C4 Filter the restaurants that can fit within the user's time window.
  • PRThe other limits: time, distance, delivery or pickup, and a budget cap. Thesisbased on users' time constraints, budget, and preferences. Cuisine is the preference the student states with one tab.
  • WS2 DC6 ranks by preference fit, price, timing. The sorts keep price and timing. Ranking by personal preference needs a user profile in the backend (C5), so it is out of the demo's scope (D1). Here the student states a preference with the cuisine tab and can sort by the like rate.
  • No source for the tabs-below-chips layout or the slider. Those are design calls, argued above.
P3

The "Filters" chip

Decision
The first chip shows a sliders icon plus "Filters", with a badge for the active count. It opens the full Filters sheet, the same as the top-right icon.
Why
On most apps "All" means "no filter", so a chip named "All" that opens a sheet would surprise people. "Filters 2" also shows at a glance how many filters are on.
Evidence
No source in the team's documents. This follows common app patterns.
P4

Budget is the all-in price

Decision
Budget is the all-in price of one meal: the item, delivery fee, small-order fee, service fee, tax and tip. Every menu row and every near meal shows it. Tap a price to see the breakdown (screen 6). Result cards show no price (P5). Tax follows the restaurant's address. The tip is 15% by default.
Small-order fee
Some places charge a fee when the food costs less than a minimum. A single cheap meal often hits it, so it is part of the all-in price. It can make a cheaper menu item cost more: the $10.75 Tofu Rice Bowl comes to $19.48, more than the $12.00 Chicken Rice Bowl at $18.73.
Why
Our research's must-fix pain point is the surprise total at checkout. Every student holds a personal price limit, and fees at checkout make them drop full carts. One interviewee put it as "the total jumped from like $13 to $24". An all-in price lets a student decide once, on the real number. This serves Value and Predictability, and it cuts decision effort: no trip to checkout to find the total. A menu-price filter would repeat the surprise.
Pickup
No delivery fee. For pickup the all-in price is the item, small-order fee, service fee, tax and tip, and the breakdown has no delivery fee line. The Tofu Rice Bowl is $19.48 for delivery and $17.49 for pickup.
Principle
Removes a step (the trip to checkout to see the total) and makes the price step cheap: one number to compare with the budget.
Evidence
  • WS2 ANeed found in research: fees at checkout drive cart abandonment.
  • PRStudents compare menus, prices and delivery times, and set a budget cap. WS2 DC2 retrieves menu, price, and fee details.
  • Not in these documents: the interview quote "the total jumped from like $13 to $24". It comes from the team's earlier interviews.
Label
Near meals say "Est. 9–14 min · 0.4 mi · $16.32 all-in": one "Est." covers the time and the price. In Pickup they say "0.4 mi · Est. $15.33 all-in". Menu rows say "Est. $18.73". The slider header says "Estimated all-in: food, fees, tax and tip". The breakdown is titled "Estimated all-in price". "Out-the-door price" is longer and comes from car buying.
Why "Est."
The price cannot be exact before checkout. The tip is the user's choice, the delivery and service fees can vary, and tax is estimated. "Est." keeps the number honest. It still shows cents, so it compares cleanly with the budget.

P5

Results are restaurant cards with the meals that fit

Experiment
Decision
Each result card is one restaurant. Its photo on top, then its name and stars, then one plain line with the estimated time range and distance: "Est. 9–14 min · 0.4 mi". The last line counts the meals that fit and names them in the sort order: "5 under $20: Soup Dumplings (8), Pork Dumplings (12), … ›". With no budget it reads "5 on the menu ›". The card shows no price and no dish rating.
Hierarchy
Place first, then speed, then the meals. Time and distance sit together because both say how soon the food can reach you. Nothing in that line is bold; a bold time would read like a promise (P9). In Pickup the line is only the distance, "0.4 mi": the time is a delivery time, so it would mislead a student who picks up.
Why places
Version 37 tries this. A restaurant card is how students already scan DoorDash, and it keeps the list short and calm: one name, one line of facts, one line of meals. The budget is still per meal: the dish line counts only meals whose all-in price fits, and the menu shows each meal's all-in price (P4, P6).
Trade-off
The price moves one tap away, onto the menu. Versions 26 to 36 put a meal and its all-in price on every card, so a student could pick without opening a menu. This version bets that "5 under $20" is enough to choose a place, and that the menu is the right place to choose the meal. The user study should compare time to place an order for both.
One per place
Each restaurant shows once, so one cheap place cannot fill the screen with five bowls. The count reads "6 places have a meal that fits".
Dish line
It counts every meal that fits, not "+N more", because no meal is shown above it. Names follow the menu's order (P6): with Lowest price, cheapest all-in first; with any other sort, most-liked first. Tap the line or the card to open the menu in budget view.
Ratings
The card shows the restaurant's stars, "4.7 ★ (900+)". Dish like rates, "88% (120)", stay on the menu next to each dish. The stars help pick the place; the like rate helps pick the meal.
Principle
The card gives the facts to pick a place: speed, stars and how many meals fit. It does not pick a meal for the student; the dish line lists them in a visible order, and the menu shows them all.
Evidence
  • WS2 BThe failure is not knowing which meals he can get. The dish line names them on each card. Tension: the job is about meals, and the card now leads with the place.
  • WS2 Astudents can't judge portion or quality from a photo; item-level reviews are stronger as data for [Quick Meal]. That is the dish like rate, now on the menu.
  • PRbombarded with a lot of options (Marcus). One card per place keeps the list short.
P6

Restaurant menu in budget view

Decision
Tapping a card or its dish line opens that restaurant's full menu. Nothing is highlighted. The first meal named on the card is the first item, because the dish line uses this view's order.
Layout
A budget bar on top: "Your budget: up to $20 est. all-in · Change". Change opens the budget sheet. Then "Under your budget (3)", in the current sort order: most-liked first, or cheapest first with Lowest price. Then "Over your budget (2)", smallest overage first, each tagged like "$0.54 over". Every row shows the dish, its like rate, the menu price and the est. all-in price.
Why one page
The student sees what fits and also what is just over. A $0.54 overage may be worth it, and a separate "fits only" list would hide it. It also keeps the restaurant's real menu as the single place to browse dishes.
No budget
The full menu in one list, in the same sort order, with an est. all-in price on every row and no sections.
Pickup
The header shows "4.6 ★ (800+) · 0.4 mi" with no time, and every price is the pickup price (P4).
Example
At Paseo Rice Bowl, the $11.50 Veggie Bowl is over a $20 budget while the $12.00 Chicken Rice Bowl fits. The small-order fee explains the gap, and tapping the price shows it.
Principle
Quick Meal shows what is just over the budget and by how much. The student decides if $0.54 is worth it. A hidden meal is a choice the app made for them.
Evidence
  • PRStudents compare menus, prices; this view does the comparing for one place. WS2 AFees drive abandonment (P4).
  • No source for showing over-budget meals. It follows from the principle.

Why one
Both filters answer one question: how soon can I get the food? For delivery, the time already includes the trip, so distance adds nothing. For pickup, the delivery time means nothing, and only the student knows if they walk, bike or drive, so they judge by distance. Two filters for one question make the student combine two numbers, and the two can conflict. That is an extra step with no new fact.
Why a switch
The switch shows that the choice is one or the other. The student flips it on purpose and sees the time slider change to a distance slider. Version 34 kept both filters because making them exclusive would "quietly clear" one. With a switch the change is not quiet: it is the student's own tap, in plain view. Nothing changes on the list until "Show results".
Rules
Switching sides clears the other side's value in the sheet, and the new slider starts at Any. The chip reads "30 min" or "Pickup · 1 mi". The Filters badge counts it as one filter. A link that has both time and distance keeps the time and drops the distance, so old links still open. The saved default (P8) keeps the side and its one value. Pickup with no distance picked is the same as no speed filter, so the sheet opens on Delivery next time.
Pickup results

Evidence
  • PRThe student's set-up includes time, distance, delivery or pickup.
  • WS2 C·8Pickup is a case the team tracks: pivot If it only works for pickup orders or merchants located very close to campus.
  • WS2 C·4The time is an ETA for food preparation and delivery, so it does not fit pickup.
P8

Filters are saved as the default

Decision
Quick Meal remembers delivery or pickup, its time or distance, and the budget, and opens with them next time. A student sets "30 min · $20" once and does not set it again.
What saves
Only choices the user makes: "Show results" in the Filters sheet or in a chip's sheet. "Clear all" plus apply saves "no filters".
What does not
Relax chips on the no-match screen change the results for this visit only. The cuisine tab is never saved and goes back to All each visit.
Why
A student's budget is a fixed number. A one-time "Budget up to $17" should not quietly raise it for good. Cuisine is the mood of one meal; a saved "Pizza" would hide everything else next time.
Principle
The biggest cut in steps: a return visit starts with the student's own limits already on. It saves only what the student chose, never a guess.
Evidence
  • PRStudents can set it up with their own preferences… After that, they can switch on [Quick Meal] with one tap; toggle their constraints with unparalleled ease.
  • No source for keeping relax chips and the cuisine tab out of the default. That follows from the principle.
Feedback
The first time filters are applied, a short toast says "Saved. Quick Meal will open with these filters." After that, the black chips are enough.
Edge cases
A link with filters in the URL wins over the saved default. A saved value outside the current range is moved to the nearest valid value.
P9

Times are estimates, not promises

Decision
Every time shows as an estimated range, "Est. 9–14 min", on cards, near meals and the menu. The time sheet says "Not guaranteed." A place fits only if the slow end of its range fits the window.
Why
Our TA asked how we could guarantee a delivery time and what happens when an order is late. There is no simple answer, so Quick Meal does not promise. Its job is to save decision time. On-time delivery is a guardrail we measure, not a promise we make. Using the slow end keeps the filter on the safe side.
Metrics
North star: time to place an order (decision time = steps × cost of each step). Guardrail: share of orders that arrive within the window the student picked. Real ranges would come from DoorDash's prep-time and Dasher data.
Targets
From the team's documents: time to order 30% lower, order completion 10% higher (thesis). Continue if at least 80% of orders arrive in the window and time to order drops 20% or orders grow 5%. Kill if under 70% arrive on time, time to order drops less than 5%, or NPS drops (Worksheet 2, Part C, field 8).
Evidence
  • WS2 C·4The time shown on the filtered option would be just an estimate, not guarantee or promise.
  • WS2 F-03Guardrail: do not label uncertain places recommended/guaranteed.
  • WS2 C·5The question to learn: Will users trust the estimated time filter. WS2 GThe risk if wrong: losing the user's trust in the estimated delivery time.
  • Tension. WS2 G says the beachhead will validate a promised time window. It should say "estimated".
P10

No subtitle; a first-visit hint explains Quick Meal

Decision
The Quick Meal page has only its title, like other DoorDash pages at the same level. When no time, distance or budget is set, a hint above the list says what Quick Meal is for: "Short on time? Pick a time and a budget to see meals that fit." The hint goes away once a filter is on.
Why
A teammate noted that "Quick Meal" can read like "fast food", and that the message should not sound budget-first. A subtitle on every visit would fix the first point, but no other DoorDash page at this level has one, and it would take a line from the list every time. The hint shows only when it helps: on a first visit, or after "Clear all". It replaces the old hint, "Set a budget to see what fits.", which put budget first.
Evidence
  • PRThe pitch leads with time: students just tell [Quick Meal] how much time they have.
  • No source in the assignment documents for the fast-food concern; it came from the team's Slack review.
Demo decisions

How this demo is built

These only shape the demo. They are not product decisions.

D1

Screen scope

Decision
Home (thin) → Quick Meal list → Filters sheet (plus a small single-filter sheet per chip) → Restaurant menu in budget view → Price breakdown sheet.
Home
Clickable but shallow. Category row, one banner, cuisine chips, a "Near campus" row. Search shows a toast: "Search is not part of this demo."
Not built
Cart, checkout, search and account. "Add to cart" shows a toast: "Cart is not part of this demo."
No preference ranking
Worksheet 2 ranks places by "preference fit" (Part D, C5 and C6; Part F-01). That needs a user profile: what each student likes, learned from past orders. The demo has no accounts and no order history, so it cannot show this honestly. A real backend would add it as one more sort the student picks, and the time and budget filters would still apply first (Part E).
Why
The demo is about deciding fast within a time window and a budget. The menu page shows the all-in prices in full, on the list and the menu, with what breaks the budget marked.
D2

Fixed location and time

Decision
The location is fixed at San José State University in downtown San Jose, CA. "Now" is the only time. Closed places never appear. The page uses the dense-area distance steps. Filter steps, the slider range and the cuisine tabs are fixed in the page.
Why
A fixed location gives the same results in every run, so the demo script and the acceptance criteria stay stable. Asking for the phone's location would add a permission prompt during a live demo and change the results by where the presenter stands.
Evidence
WS2 GThe beachhead is Bay Area students: Berkley (45K) + Stanford (17K) + SJSU(36K).
D3

Mock data, branding and photos

Decision
Realistic mock data with made-up restaurant names near campus. DoorDash-like red and layout, but no DoorDash logo. Footer line: "Concept demo for a CMU course. Not affiliated with DoorDash."
Realism
Menu prices, delivery fees and wait times follow what students see near the campus. The cuisine mix matches the area. A few places are "close but slow" or "far but fast", such as 4th St. Noodle Bar (0.3 mi, 19 min) and Santa Clara Taco Co. (0.9 mi, 16 min), so Delivery and Pickup give different lists.
Photos
About 15 to 20 free food photos (Unsplash or Pexels license), one per dish type such as bowl, noodles, burger or taco, shared by similar dishes. Compressed and bundled. Fallback: colored tiles with the dish type.
Why
DoorDash's US consumer terms, section 5 (p) to (r), ban collecting its text, photos or data by automated means or by "systematic retrieval", and its photos belong to DoorDash or the restaurants. The demo also lives on a public URL, where real store names next to made-up ratings, prices and wait times would show false facts about real businesses.
Evidence
WS2 C·6The smallest test: Manually collect a list of restaurants near campus and calculate the estimated delivery time. The mock data is that list.
D4

Fee, tax and time values

Service fee
15%. This matches DoorDash's published California rate for orders without DashPass (5% with DashPass). The demo prices every meal as a non-member, the case after a student's free DashPass year ends.
Tax
One assumed rate of 9.5% for every place, stored per restaurant so the formula already works per address.
Small-order fee
$2.50 when the food costs less than $12. Assumed value. It applies to pickup too.
Delivery fee
Set per restaurant. Delivery only; pickup has none.
Tip
15%, the default the user would see.
Rounding
All math in integer cents, each line rounded half up, so every breakdown adds up to the cent.
Time range
The range runs from the restaurant's ETA minus 5 minutes to its ETA, for example 9–14 min for a 14-minute ETA. Filters and sorting use the ETA, the slow end. A real backend would return its own range.
D5

Build choices

Saved filters
Stored in the phone's browser (local storage). The demo has no login, so a server copy would still be tied to one browser. If the browser blocks storage (some private modes), Quick Meal opens with no saved filters and shows no error.
Budget slider
A native range input with custom styling. It works with touch, mouse and keyboard, and screen readers read its value.
No-match results

When nothing matches

The API always returns three lists. The page picks the layout from them, so there is no special code path for "empty".

GET /api/quick-meal/search?budget=15&time=15

{
  "filters": { "budget": 15, "time": 15, "distance": null, "cuisine": null, "sort": "fastest" },
  "exact": [],
  "near": [
    { "restaurantId": "santa-clara-taco-co", "restaurant": "Santa Clara Taco Co.",
      "dish": "Chicken Tacos (2)", "allIn": 12.86, "etaMin": 16, "distanceMi": 0.9,
      "miss": [ { "filter": "time", "over": 1, "label": "1 min slower" } ] },
    { "restaurantId": "taylor-st-dumplings", "dish": "Pork Dumplings (12)",
      "miss": [ { "filter": "budget", "over": 1.32, "label": "$1.32 over budget" } ] }
  ],
  "relax": [
    { "filter": "budget", "to": 17, "count": 1, "label": "Budget up to $17 · 1 result" },
    { "filter": "time",   "to": 20, "count": 2, "label": "Time up to 20 min · 2 results" }
  ]
}
  • Exact. One card per open place that passes every active filter, with the meals that fit. Near meals stay meal cards: each one says how that meal misses, and a miss like "$1.32 over budget" belongs to a dish.
  • Near. Only filled when exact is empty. For each open place, take its meal that misses least. Score the miss: budget overshoot ÷ $5, time overshoot ÷ 10 min, distance overshoot ÷ 1 mi, then add them up. Show the 5 lowest scores. Each card lists what it misses in plain words.
  • Cuisine stays fixed. Near meals only miss on budget, time or distance, never on cuisine. A student who picked Indian never sees a burger as "close". If no place of that cuisine is nearby at all, the relax chip reads "All cuisines · N results" and tapping it switches to the All tab.
  • Relax. One suggestion per active filter, with the others held. Budget is a slider, so it suggests the lowest whole-dollar budget that gives at least one result ($16.32 rounds up to $17). Time and distance move to the next stop that gives a result. If no single change helps, offer "Remove time" (or "Remove distance" for pickup). Tapping a chip applies it, moves the slider to match, and shows the new results.
  • Copy. Title "No exact matches". Body "Nothing fits a $15 budget and 15 min. Here are the closest meals." No apology, no dead end.
  • Pickup. Near meals show the distance and no time, miss only on budget or distance ("0.4 mi farther"), and use pickup prices.
  • Principle. The miss labels make each near meal a cheap step: the student sees "1 min slower" and judges it. Relax chips loosen a filter only when tapped.
  • Evidence. PRIf nothing fits, [Quick Meal] reports back truthfully and suggests the fastest options. WS2 F-02clearly indicate that no option fits the time window and show the closest alternatives with clearly labelling the estimated time. With only time set, the closest meals are the fastest ones; with a budget too, a meal can also be close on price.

The page itself is deterministic: the same filters give the same list, and the time and budget filters are hard rules that no score can override (Part E). Backend

API

All read-only GET calls with query strings. Easy to test in a browser and easy to show in slides. Everything new lives under /api/quick-meal/. Existing restaurant and menu APIs stay as they are; Quick Meal reads their data and adds the estimated all-in prices on top. Other screens never see a change.

RouteReturns
GET /api/homeCategories, banner, cuisine chips, "Near campus" row.
GET /api/quick-meal/searchParams budget, time or distance (one of the two; if both come, time wins), cuisine (one value, empty for All), sort. Returns exact (one entry per place: its first fitting meal and the names of the others; the card shows the place and those names), near, relax, and total. It runs when the user applies filters or taps a tab.
GET /api/quick-meal/restaurants/:id?budget=One restaurant and its full menu in budget view. With distance set, it uses pickup prices and the page shows no time. Each item has its est. all-in price, the breakdown lines, and, when a budget is given, "fits" or how much it is over.

For the presentation

Demo script, about 2 minutes

  1. Home. Tap Quick Meal.
  2. Tap Time ▾, slide to 30 min and apply: 17 places. Point out that every time is an estimated range, not a promise.
  3. Tap Budget ▾. Slide to $25 and apply: 13 places. Open it again, slide to $20 and apply: 6 places.
  4. Scroll to the Paseo Rice Bowl card and tap "3 under $20". The menu shows 3 bowls that fit and 2 that do not. The Tofu Rice Bowl is cheaper on the menu, but tap its price: the small-order fee makes it $19.48, more than the $18.73 Chicken Rice Bowl.
  5. Go back. Open the Filters sheet and tighten to 15 min and $15. Show the no-match state and how each meal misses: the Chicken Tacos are only "1 min slower", and the student judges that. Tap "Budget up to $17 · 1 result". The slider moves to $17 and one place shows.
  6. Open the Filters sheet and flip Delivery to Pickup. The time slider changes to a distance slider: one or the other, never both. Slide to 1 mi and tap "Show results": 5 places. The cards show the distance and no time. Open a menu: each price is lower by the delivery fee.
